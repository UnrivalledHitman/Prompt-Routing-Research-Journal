\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Effects Occur}
\label{sec:why}

\emph{Stickiness is set by the cost distribution.} A policy that never moves
pays the cost of its cell less the cost of $\cstar(q)$, so its relative tax
is the ratio of the two costs less one. All three pools share a shape that
makes that ratio large: the top rung combines the largest model with its
costliest setting, while the mass of $\cstar(q)$ sits at the other end,
among the cheapest one or two options. The tax therefore follows how far
the top rung's price sits above the options that answer most prompts,
which is 14 times locally, nine on the public pool and five on the hosted
ladder.

\emph{Escalation is small when the ladder's cost order matches the order
of sufficiency.} Each failed rung is paid in full, so a climb is cheap only
if its early rungs are both cheap and likely to succeed. The local P1
ladder has that property: its first four rungs are the four cheapest cells,
and they are $\cstar(q)$ for 89.2\% of prompts, so the climb meets the
minimum sufficient cell in almost the order an oracle would try the cells.
P2 breaks it at its second rung, where thinking at 0.6B costs more than any
larger non-thinking cell and answers fewer prompts than any of them. The
same property explains the other pools: the hosted ladder's cheapest cell
suffices for three queries in four, while on the public pool 29.8\% of
queries are answered most cheaply by a model the frontier ladder omits, so
the climb cannot stop at $\cstar(q)$.

\emph{The costliest configuration is not the most accurate.} This holds in
every pool we measured on tasks with a checkable answer
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-labels}, \ref{sec:results-public}
and~\ref{sec:results-hosted}). Where we can see a mechanism, it is the
generation budget: between 16\% and 20\% of local thinking generations, and
18.9\% of \texttt{gpt-5-nano}'s at high effort, run out of tokens before
they answer, and a truncated answer fails. A budget is part of any deployed
configuration, so sticky use of the top rung pays twice, once in cost and
again in the answers it loses. Open-ended requests are the exception: there
the cap rarely binds and $c_8$ is the most accurate cell
(Section~\ref{sec:results-open}), yet sticky use of it still pays 5.39 times
the minimum sufficient energy.

\emph{Thinking is priced by length, and length varies by family.} Energy per
output token barely moves with the mode (Section~\ref{sec:results-mode}), so
the mode premium is the ratio of output lengths, scaled by the change in
accuracy. Plain math answers already write out their working, so thinking
lengthens them about five times and the energy ratio stays between 4.9 and
6.1. Plain code and multiple-choice answers are shorter, as few as 30 tokens
for multiple choice at 0.6B, thinking lengthens them 7 to 25 times, and
their ratios reach 15.8 and 15.2; they fall only where thinking also adds
many answers, as on code at 1.7B. The same accounting shows why a larger
model beats thinking below 8B: a size step keeps the output about as long
and raises energy per generation by 1.4 to 2.7 times, while a mode step
keeps the model and makes the output seven or eight times longer.

\emph{Routing too high is mostly a failure too.} Of the 968.6\,J premium
for routing one tier too high, only 167.5\,J is the higher tier's own excess
cost. The other 801.1\,J is recovery, because a step up in energy order
is not always a step up in accuracy (the tier above $c_7$ is 0.6B with
thinking), and the step up fails on 24.1\% of prompts. Against overshoot
alone, routing too low would cost 15.7 times as much, an order of
magnitude. The factor of 2.71 in Section~\ref{sec:results-asymmetry} is
lower because the grid is not monotone: a costlier tier is not a safer
one. That factor also depends on recovering at $c_8$, the costliest cell,
and a cheaper recovery would narrow it.

\emph{A router loses to the frugal climb in recovery, not in prediction
alone.} P1's failures are cheap because its early rungs are the cheapest
cells. A router's failure is expensive because it recovers at $c_8$, which
costs eight times $c_7$ and answers fewer prompts. A router can avoid that
only by routing high, and the selected threshold does: the best router sends
three answerable prompts in four to a costlier cell than they need, so its
tax is almost all stickiness. Recovering by climbing from the routed cell
brings every router within 6\% of P1. The oracle's 32\% saving over P1
therefore needs a router that is rarely wrong, and the best router we
trained picks $\cstar(q)$ exactly for one prompt in seven.

\emph{Router cost does not separate the families on this grid; training data
does.} Every router's own energy is under 1\% of what it routes, so charging
it leaves the energy ranking almost untouched. The order comes instead from
how well each family learns from 716 training prompts. A linear model on
$n$-grams or frozen embeddings needs little data, and the fine-tuned
encoders came close to it only when cost-sensitive weights focused training
on the errors that cost most. A 0.6B language model prompted to name the
cheapest sufficient configuration routes low, and on this grid routing low
is the expensive error (Section~\ref{sec:results-asymmetry}). Trained with
LoRA on the zero-shot prompt it spends a fifth less than prompted zero-shot,
but stays behind the linear routers. Only in tokens, where
R3 pays for reading its own prompt, does charging a router reorder the
families.

\emph{Generating first did not pay for the cascade.} Its checks cost 9.7\,J
each, so verification is not what makes it expensive. The information is
weak: a small model checking its own answer accepts a wrong one on a third
of answerable prompts, and a wrong answer that passes the check costs as
much as a misroute. The cascade therefore pays P1's escalation and a
router's recovery together. A stronger verifier, or AutoMix's
meta-verifier, which we omit, could change that
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations below are grouped by what they threaten: the comparator,
the labels, the cost measurements, and how far the findings generalise.

\emph{The comparator is constructed.} The manual escalation policies of
Table~\ref{tab:policies} are not observed behaviour. We have no logs of how
users of language-model services move between configurations, so the
ladders, the starting distribution of P4 and the detection probabilities
$p_{\mathrm{acc}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{rej}}$ are all assumptions. We limit the
damage in three ways: by reporting a family of policies that ranges from
frugal to wasteful, by quoting headline figures under oracle detection,
in which the manual policies answer the most prompts, and by sweeping the
detection probabilities instead of fixing them. The tax we report is therefore the
tax these policies would pay. It is not a measurement of what real users
pay, and it is the part of the study that most needs evidence from outside
it. Two of these choices favour the frugal climb, the policy the routers
fail to beat. Oracle detection assumes a user who recognises every wrong
answer, which is generous on mathematics and code and implausible on
open-ended work, and the climb makes the most attempts at which such a user
could be fooled: at the illustrative detection point it answers 12.3 points
fewer prompts. Model loading is also uncharged, and the climb changes model
more often than any router.

\emph{Attempts are independent single-turn requests, and loading is not
charged.} Each rung of a ladder is costed by \eqref{eq:policycost} as a
fresh request carrying only the prompt, and it succeeds or fails as that
cell did during labelling. A user who escalates within one conversation also
resends the earlier answers, which lengthens every retry, and the stronger
configuration may be helped or misled by seeing a failed attempt; neither
effect is modelled, and the first would make escalation more expensive than
we report. Labelling is also model-major, and a window in which any
generation reloaded the model is rerun (Section~\ref{sec:energy}), so every
cost is that of a model already in memory. On an 8\,GB GPU that holds one
model at a time, a user who escalates across sizes, or a router that sends
consecutive queries to different sizes, also pays the time and energy of
loading one. Our costs understate both, and they understate most for
whichever policy or router changes size most often.

\emph{Labels come from one sample under fixed caps.} With $k = 1$, each
$s(q, c)$ is a single draw of a stochastic outcome, and $\cstar(q)$ inherits
its noise. On the noise subset a cell's label differs between two samples
8.9\% of the time, $\cstar(q)$ in tokens agrees between two samples for
72.0\% of prompts, and the single-sample $\cstar(q)$ matches the majority of
three for 84.0\% (Section~\ref{sec:decoding}). The bootstrap, however, resamples
prompts with their labels held fixed, so its intervals do not include that
uncertainty. The labels also depend on the generation caps. A thinking
answer cut off at 4{,}096 tokens counts as a failure, which is the right
rule for deployment but makes thinking cells look worse than they would with
a longer budget. That budget is below what Qwen3's own guidance suggests for
thinking mode, and it truncates 16 to 20\% of thinking generations, so it
shapes three results that would otherwise read as properties of the models:
that $c_8$ answers fewer prompts than $c_7$, that thinking is never the
cheaper way to buy accuracy, and part of P3's tax. The rerun bounds the
effect rather than removing it: a longer cap moved $\cstar(q)$ for 6.4\% of
the 219 capped generations rerun, and 27.9\% of them reached the
8{,}192-token cap too (Section~\ref{sec:results-mode}). What the grid shows
is how configurations behave under a deployment budget, not how the models
behave without one. Finally, the labels are tied to the recommended sampling
settings of each mode, and other settings could change them.

\emph{Grading is imperfect.} Math answers are compared after extraction and
normalisation, which can reject a correct answer written in an unusual form.
An unparseable multiple-choice answer counts as a failure even when the
intended option is clear to a reader. Code is judged by the datasets' own
unit tests, which can pass an incorrect function. The open-ended family
depends on a model judge that agrees with a human only moderately, at
$\kappa = 0.42$, and passes more answers than either the human or
\texttt{gpt-5-mini} (Section~\ref{sec:judge}). Its results are reported
separately and with that caveat, and every other result covers the gradable
families. Re-grading the family with \texttt{gpt-5-mini}, which agrees with
the human at $\kappa = 0.74$, would tighten it.

\emph{The energy boundary is the GPU board.} NVML reports GPU board power,
so our joules exclude the processor, system memory, storage and display,
all of which draw power while a query is served. Each query's energy is
also apportioned rather than measured: reported power refreshes only every
0.5\,s, so a window's dynamic energy is shared among its queries by
generation time, with token share as a check. CPU routers are charged an
upper bound on their energy, not a measurement. That bound works against R1
in any comparison with a router measured on the GPU.

\emph{One model family, quantised, up to 8B.} Every cell is a Q4\_K\_M
quantisation of a Qwen3 model, so size comparisons are between quantised
models, and quantisation need not degrade every size equally. The largest
cell has 8B parameters. Thinking is either on or off, although Qwen3 also
accepts a thinking budget, and the separation of the two modes was checked
on 60 prompts per size with a lexical proxy
(Section~\ref{sec:gate-mode}). Hosted frontier tiers differ from this pool
in capability, in price and in how reasoning effort is exposed. The
public-benchmark arm (Section~\ref{sec:public-arm}) tests whether the tax
analysis carries over to such pools, and the hosted-frontier arm
(Section~\ref{sec:hosted-arm}) restores the mode axis there with reasoning
effort as a paid setting. Neither has energy data.

\emph{The hosted arm is priced, not metered.} Its costs are one provider's
list prices on one date, halved by the batch discount, and a price change
moves every hosted figure. The provider reports the reasoning tokens it
bills for but not the energy behind them, and its high-effort cells carry
the local grid's cap rule, which truncates a minority of their generations;
that is why each hosted cell is reported at two accuracies
(Section~\ref{sec:hosted-arm}).

\emph{One machine.} All measurements come from one laptop and one GPU.
Other workloads that share the machine are kept out of the measurements by
waiting for the GPU to be idle, not by isolating it. Latency and energy
depend on this silicon, driver and serving stack, and we do not claim that
the figures transfer to other hardware.

\emph{Public, English, single-turn prompts.} The gradable families come from
public benchmarks, in English and single-turn. Such benchmarks may appear
in the models' training data, which would raise success rates across the
grid; how far that would change which cell is cheapest is unknown.
Benchmark prompts are also more uniform than live traffic, which is one
reason the corpus includes an open-ended family.

\emph{The comparators are simplified.} The cascade follows AutoMix without
its POMDP meta-verifier, reads its confidence from a single forward pass,
and climbs only the P1 ladder. The prompted R3 routers read their choice from
next-token probabilities without training, and the LoRA variant was trained
on 716 prompts. Both simplifications may understate what a stronger cascade
or a language-model router trained on more data could achieve. All routers are trained and evaluated on one split, and $\tau$ is
fixed on its validation part, so the reported intervals exclude the
uncertainty of that choice (Section~\ref{sec:stats}). The data are also thin
for the comparison between router families: 716 training prompts, 243 test
prompts and labels from one sample per cell. A linear model on $n$-grams
needs little of either, while fine-tuned encoders and LoRA adapters are the
methods most likely to improve with more; their ranking here should be read
as what these families achieve at this scale, not as a general ordering.

\subsection{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

\emph{Defaults decide most of the tax.} Stickiness is between 74\% and all
of the tax for the policies that often start high (P3, both variants of P4
and random assignment), and in all three pools most prompts are answered
by the cheapest options. A service that opens on its
smallest adequate model, with extended reasoning off and a one-step-up
control in view, puts its users on the frugal climb. That climb spends 1.5
times the oracle's energy per solved prompt on the local grid, against 5.7
times for leaving the largest thinking cell selected. Turning reasoning on
by default is the harder choice to defend. Within the cap it lost accuracy
at 4B and 8B and was never the cheaper way to buy accuracy on the gradable
families; at \texttt{gpt-5} on the hosted ladder, high effort answered 4.1\%
of queries that minimal effort missed, at 7.26 times the price, and moved
overall accuracy by a tenth of a point. Reasoning is worth paying for
on a minority of prompts, which argues for choosing it per prompt, not
leaving it selected. The default moves carbon as well as cost. Replacing
P3's habit with P1's shifts the excess emissions about as far as moving
the workload from a coal-heavy grid to a low-carbon one
(Section~\ref{sec:results-carbon}).

\emph{A user without a router should change size before mode.} On this
evidence the cheapest habit is to start small with thinking off, move up a
size when an answer fails, and turn thinking on only when the largest size
without it has failed. That advice depends on recognising a wrong answer. A
user who accepts one wrong answer in five keeps the frugal climb's low cost
but loses the extra answers it buys, and ends up answering about as many
prompts as a user who never left the largest cell
(Section~\ref{sec:results-tax}). Prompts on which the user cannot check the
answer are where a one-shot router has most to offer, since its choice does
not depend on the user's judgment of the output.

\emph{Cost should be reported in the unit that is paid, per solved prompt.}
Output tokens price the mode axis to within about 15\% but miss the factor
of 6.6 in energy per token between 0.6B and 8B, so in tokens P3 looks
cheaper than P2 and routing too low looks cheaper than routing too high,
and energy reverses both. A study that prices a mixed pool in tokens
can therefore favour exactly the misallocations that cost most. The public
and hosted pools also show negative taxes for static policies that underpay
by failing, so a tax, like a cost, means little without the solved rate
beside it.

\emph{Deploy a one-shot router against habits, not against a disciplined
climb.} Charged for its own energy, the best router spends 61\% less per
prompt than leaving the 8B thinking cell selected and 29\% less than users
who start at a uniformly random cell, answering more prompts than the first
and about as many as the second. It does not beat a user who climbs sizes
with thinking off and recognises every failure. Two conditions favour it:
when failures are not always recognised it answers 7.1 points more than the
climb, because it makes fewer attempts at which to accept a wrong answer,
and when its failures are recovered by climbing rather than by jumping to
the largest cell it comes within 6\% of the climb's energy. A service that
routes should therefore recover by climbing, and it gains most for users who
cannot check answers.
