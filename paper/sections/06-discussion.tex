\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Effects Occur}
\label{sec:why}

\emph{Stickiness is set by the cost distribution.} A policy that never moves
pays the cost of its cell less the cost of $\cstar(q)$, so P3's relative
tax is the ratio of the two costs less one. On the local grid $c_8$ costs
14 times the mean minimum sufficient cost. Two things make that ratio
large: the top cell combines the largest model with thinking, and the mass
of $\cstar(q)$ sits at the other end, where one of the two smallest
non-thinking cells is $\cstar(q)$ for 60\% of prompts. The other two pools
share the shape. On the public pool one of the two cheapest models is the
cheapest sufficient choice for 46.5\% of queries, and P3's rung costs nine
times the mean minimum sufficient cost. On the hosted ladder the cheapest
cell is that choice for 74.2\%, and the top rung costs five times as
much. The tax is large in all
three pools for the same reason, and its size follows how far the top
rung's price sits above the cells that answer most prompts.

\emph{Escalation is small when the ladder's cost order matches the order
of sufficiency.} Each failed rung is paid in full, so a climb is cheap only
if its early rungs are both cheap and likely to succeed. The local P1
ladder has that property. Its first four rungs are the four cheapest cells,
and they are $\cstar(q)$ for 89.2\% of prompts, so the climb meets the
minimum sufficient cell in almost the order an oracle would try the cells.
P2 breaks the property at its second rung. Thinking at 0.6B costs more
than any of the three larger non-thinking cells and answers fewer prompts
than any of them. The same property explains the other two pools. On the
hosted ladder the cheapest cell suffices for three queries in four, and P1
pays 0.22 times the minimum sufficient cost. On the public pool 29.8\% of
queries are answered most cheaply by a model the frontier ladder does not
contain, so for those the climb cannot stop at $\cstar(q)$, and P1 pays
2.69 times.

\emph{The costliest configuration is not the most accurate.} This holds in
every pool we measured. Locally, $c_7$ answers more prompts than $c_8$. On
the public pool, nine of the eleven models dearer than
\texttt{qwen3-235b-a22b-2507} answer fewer queries than it does. On the
hosted ladder, \texttt{gpt-5} at high effort answers fewer than
\texttt{gpt-5-mini} at high effort. Where we can see a mechanism, it is
the generation budget. Between 16\% and 20\% of local thinking generations,
and 18.9\% of \texttt{gpt-5-nano}'s at high effort, run out of tokens
before they answer, and a truncated answer fails. A budget is part of any
deployed configuration, so sticky use of the top rung pays twice: once in
cost, and again in the answers it loses.

\emph{Thinking is priced by length, and length varies by family.} Energy per
output token barely moves with the mode (Section~\ref{sec:results-mode}),
so the mode premium is the ratio of output lengths, scaled by the change in
accuracy. Plain math answers already write out their working, about 430
output tokens at both 0.6B and 8B. Thinking lengthens them about five
times, and on math the energy ratio stays between 4.9 and 6.1. Plain code
and multiple-choice answers are shorter, 100 to 240 tokens and as few as 30
for multiple choice at 0.6B, and thinking lengthens them 7 to 25 times.
Their ratios reach 15.8 on code and 15.2 on multiple choice, and they fall
only where thinking also adds many answers. On code at 1.7B it lifts
accuracy from 31.0\% to 47.3\%, and the ratio drops to 6.4. The same
accounting shows why a larger model beats thinking below 8B. A size step
keeps the output about as long and raises energy per generation by 1.4 to
2.7 times; a mode step keeps the model and makes the output seven or eight
times longer.

\emph{Routing too high is mostly a failure too.} Of the 968.6\,J premium
for routing one tier too high, only 167.5\,J is the higher tier's own excess
cost. The other 801.1\,J is recovery, because a step up in energy order
is not always a step up in accuracy (the tier above $c_7$ is 0.6B with
thinking), and the step up fails on 24.1\% of prompts. Against overshoot
alone, routing too low would cost 15.7 times as much, an order of
magnitude. The factor of 2.71 in Section~\ref{sec:results-asymmetry} is
lower because the grid is not monotone: a costlier tier is not a safer
one. That factor also depends on recovering at $c_8$, the costliest cell,
and a cheaper recovery would narrow it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations below are grouped by what they threaten: the comparator,
the labels, the cost measurements, and how far the findings generalise.

\emph{The comparator is constructed.} The manual escalation policies of
Table~\ref{tab:policies} are not observed behaviour. We have no logs of how
users of language-model services move between configurations, so the
ladders, the starting distribution of P4 and the detection probabilities
$p_{\mathrm{acc}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{rej}}$ are all assumptions. We limit the
damage in three ways: by reporting a family of policies that ranges from
frugal to wasteful, by quoting headline figures under oracle detection,
in which the manual policies answer the most prompts, and by sweeping the
detection probabilities instead of fixing them. The tax we report is therefore the
tax these policies would pay. It is not a measurement of what real users
pay, and it is the part of the study that most needs evidence from outside
it.

\emph{Attempts are independent single-turn requests.} Each rung of a ladder
is costed by \eqref{eq:policycost} as a fresh request carrying only the
prompt, and it succeeds or fails as that cell did during labelling. A user
who escalates within one conversation also resends the earlier answers,
which lengthens every retry, and the stronger configuration may be helped
or misled by seeing a failed attempt. Neither effect is modelled. The first
would make escalation more expensive than we report; the direction of the
second is unknown.

\emph{Model loading is not charged.} Labelling is model-major, and a window
in which any generation reloaded the model is rerun
(Section~\ref{sec:energy}), so every cost is that of a model already in
memory. On an 8\,GB GPU that holds one model at a time, a user who escalates
across sizes, or a router that sends consecutive queries to different sizes,
also pays the time and energy of loading a model. Our costs understate both,
and they understate most for whichever policy or router changes size most
often.

\emph{Labels come from one sample under fixed caps.} With $k = 1$, each
$s(q, c)$ is a single draw of a stochastic outcome, and $\cstar(q)$ inherits
its noise. The noise subset measures how often a label changes on
resampling (Section~\ref{sec:decoding}). The bootstrap, however, resamples
prompts with their labels held fixed, so its intervals do not include that
uncertainty. The labels also depend on the generation caps. A thinking
answer cut off at 4{,}096 tokens counts as a failure, which is the right
rule for deployment but makes thinking cells look worse than they would with
a longer budget. The cap reruns measure how often a longer cap would move
$\cstar(q)$, but on a sample of capped generations rather than on all of
them. Finally, the labels are tied to the recommended sampling settings of
each mode, and other settings could change them.

\emph{Grading is imperfect.} Math answers are compared after extraction and
normalisation, which can reject a correct answer written in an unusual form.
An unparseable multiple-choice answer counts as a failure even when the
intended option is clear to a reader. Code is judged by the datasets' own
unit tests, which can pass an incorrect function. The open-ended family
depends on a model judge whose agreement with a stronger model and with
human labels bounds how far its labels can be trusted
(Section~\ref{sec:judge}), and results cover the gradable families unless
stated otherwise.

\emph{The energy boundary is the GPU board.} NVML reports GPU board power,
so our joules exclude the processor, system memory, storage and display,
all of which draw power while a query is served. Each query's energy is
also apportioned rather than measured: reported power refreshes only every
0.5\,s, so a window's dynamic energy is shared among its queries by
generation time, with token share as a check. CPU routers are charged an
upper bound on their energy, not a measurement. That bound works against R1
in any comparison with a router measured on the GPU.

\emph{One model family, quantised, up to 8B.} Every cell is a Q4\_K\_M
quantisation of a Qwen3 model, so size comparisons are between quantised
models, and quantisation need not degrade every size equally. The largest
cell has 8B parameters. Thinking is either on or off, although Qwen3 also
accepts a thinking budget, and the separation of the two modes was checked
on 60 prompts per size with a lexical proxy
(Section~\ref{sec:gate-mode}). Hosted frontier tiers differ from this pool
in capability, in price and in how reasoning effort is exposed. The
public-benchmark arm (Section~\ref{sec:public-arm}) tests whether the tax
analysis carries over to such pools, and the hosted-frontier arm
(Section~\ref{sec:hosted-arm}) restores the mode axis there with reasoning
effort as a paid setting. Neither has energy data.

\emph{The hosted arm is priced, not metered.} Its costs are one provider's
list prices on one date, halved by the batch discount, and a price change
moves every hosted figure. The provider reports the reasoning tokens it
bills for but not the energy behind them, and its high-effort cells carry
the local grid's cap rule, which truncates a minority of their generations;
that is why each hosted cell is reported at two accuracies
(Section~\ref{sec:hosted-arm}).

\emph{One machine.} All measurements come from one laptop and one GPU.
Other workloads that share the machine are kept out of the measurements by
waiting for the GPU to be idle, not by isolating it. Latency and energy
depend on this silicon, driver and serving stack, and we do not claim that
the figures transfer to other hardware.

\emph{Public, English, single-turn prompts.} The gradable families come from
public benchmarks, in English and single-turn. Such benchmarks may appear
in the models' training data, which would raise success rates across the
grid; how far that would change which cell is cheapest is unknown.
Benchmark prompts are also more uniform than live traffic, which is one
reason the corpus includes an open-ended family.

\emph{The comparators are simplified.} The cascade follows AutoMix without
its POMDP meta-verifier, reads its confidence from a single forward pass,
and climbs only the P1 ladder. R3 reads its choice from next-token
probabilities and is not trained for routing. Both simplifications may
understate what a stronger cascade or a trained language-model router could
achieve. All routers are trained and evaluated on one split, and $\tau$ is
fixed on its validation part, so the reported intervals exclude the
uncertainty of that choice (Section~\ref{sec:stats}).

\subsection{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

\emph{Defaults decide most of the tax.} Stickiness is between 74\% and all
of the tax for the policies that often start high (P3, both variants of P4
and random assignment), and in all three pools most prompts are answered
by the cheapest options. A service that opens on its
smallest adequate model, with extended reasoning off and a one-step-up
control in view, puts its users on the frugal climb. That climb spends 1.5
times the oracle's energy per solved prompt on the local grid, against 5.7
times for leaving the largest thinking cell selected. Turning reasoning on
by default is the harder choice to defend. On the local grid thinking lost
accuracy at 4B and 8B within the cap, and it was never the cheaper way to
buy accuracy. At \texttt{gpt-5} on the hosted ladder, high effort answered
4.1\% of queries that minimal effort missed, at 7.26 times the price, and
overall accuracy moved by a tenth of a point. Reasoning is worth paying for
on a minority of prompts, which argues for choosing it per prompt, not
leaving it selected. The default moves carbon as well as cost. Replacing
P3's habit with P1's shifts the excess emissions about as far as moving
the workload from a coal-heavy grid to a low-carbon one
(Section~\ref{sec:results-carbon}).

\emph{A user without a router should change size before mode.} On this
evidence the cheapest habit is to start small with thinking off, move up a
size when an answer fails, and turn thinking on only when the largest size
without it has failed. That advice depends on recognising a wrong answer. A
user who accepts one wrong answer in five keeps the frugal climb's low cost
but loses the extra answers it buys, and ends up answering about as many
prompts as a user who never left the largest cell
(Section~\ref{sec:results-tax}). Prompts on which the user cannot check the
answer are where a one-shot router has most to offer, since its choice does
not depend on the user's judgment of the output.

\emph{Cost should be reported in the unit that is paid, per solved prompt.}
Output tokens price the mode axis to within about 15\% but miss the factor
of 6.6 in energy per token between 0.6B and 8B. Measured in tokens, P3
looks cheaper than P2 and routing too low looks cheaper than routing too
high, and energy reverses both. A study that prices a mixed pool in tokens
can therefore favour exactly the misallocations that cost most. The public
and hosted pools also show negative taxes for static policies that underpay
by failing, so a tax, like a cost, means little without the solved rate
beside it.
